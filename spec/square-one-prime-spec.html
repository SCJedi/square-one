Square One PrimeA deterministic modern console, and what it keeps from the small one

The free console is 128 x 128 and fits on a four-dollar chip. This is the other one: full resolution, full colour, real audio, carts measured in gigabytes. It keeps every property that made the small machine worth building — replays as proof, content-addressed carts, reproducible builds, a sandbox a cart cannot argue with — by moving exactly one boundary.

0. The one decision this document turns on

Everything else follows from a single line, so it goes first.

The normative boundary is the simulation, not the picture

The simulation is bit-exact and normative. Presentation is neither. Two conformant Prime machines given the same cart, the same seed and the same inputs MUST reach identical simulation state on every tick. They are under no obligation to produce identical pixels, and on real hardware they will not.

On the small console the conformance chain hashes the framebuffer, which works because a 128 x 128 software rasterizer is deterministic by construction. Put a GPU under it and that collapses immediately: drivers differ, vendors differ, the same driver differs across a version bump. A specification that demanded identical pixels from a modern renderer would be a specification nobody could implement twice.

So the line moves. And it moves to somewhere the small console already put one: audio has always been non-normative — the simulation emits commands, the mixer consumes them, nothing flows back, and mixing quality may differ between a phone and a desktop without any replay diverging. Prime applies that same rule to rendering, which is the generalisation it was always waiting for.

Normative — bit-exact, foreverNon-normative
Simulation state at the end of every tick; the PRNG sequence; all arithmetic performed by the simulation; input sampling and quantization; the order of every iteration the simulation performs; cart identity Rendering, at any fidelity; shaders; resolution and colour depth; frame interpolation; audio mixing; texture filtering; asset streaming order; anything running on a thread other than the simulation's
What this buys, and why it is worth the whole document

Replays, rollback netplay, save states, verifiable scores and time-travel debugging are all the same mechanism, and that mechanism survives intact from a four-dollar microcontroller to a machine with a discrete GPU. A replay recorded on one runs on the other. A speedrun set on Prime verifies on the free console and the reverse, because they agree about the only thing that has to be agreed about.

It also means a Prime runtime may be rewritten — new renderer, new backend, a port to hardware that did not exist when the cart shipped — without invalidating a single recorded run. The picture is an implementation detail. The simulation is the contract.

1. Overview

Prime is a first-party console: a machine we build to ship our own games on, with a cart format, a runtime contract, and a determinism guarantee. It is not a stretched version of the 128 x 128 machine and carries no obligation to run its carts.

What it inherits is the architecture rather than the aesthetic:

  • A cart is a pure function of seed and inputs. No wall clock, no ambient entropy, no network, no host storage outside its own save region.
  • A cart's identity is its content hash, and a recipe rebuilds it byte-identically.
  • A cart runs sandboxed and cannot reach the network by construction rather than by policy.
  • Modules and recipes — engines, art, audio and tuning as versioned, content-addressed layers that a stamper resolves, type-checks and proves.

What changes is scale, and one boundary.

Tier A

2. The frozen core

Blast radius: every cart ever made and every replay in the archive. Nothing here may change after Prime 1 ships.

2.1 The cart contract

A cart MUST expose:

boot(seed: u64)          once, after the cart's initial state is installed
tick(input: InputFrame)  exactly once per simulated frame
render(alpha: f64)       zero or more times per tick, on the presentation clock

render is where the split lives. It MUST NOT write to simulation state, and a conformant runtime MUST enforce that: it is called with the simulation arena mapped read-only. alpha is the interpolation fraction between the last two ticks, and it exists so a 144 Hz display looks smooth without the simulation ever running at 144 Hz.

Gotcha — interpolation must not feed back

The single most common way a deterministic engine stops being deterministic is a value computed for smoothing that finds its way into the next tick. Read-only enforcement is not a nicety here; it is the wall that keeps render from quietly becoming part of the simulation. Where the hardware cannot map it read-only, the runtime MUST hash the arena before and after render in development builds and fault on a change.

2.2 Time

The simulation runs at a fixed 60 Hz. The only clock a cart can see is a u64 tick counter. Presentation is decoupled and may run at any rate; the runtime MUST clamp catch-up to at most 5 ticks per presented frame.

No wall clock, no frame duration, no real elapsed time is reachable from a cart. As on the small console, this is what makes a cart archival: it behaves identically in 2026 and in 2050, and it cannot break on a leap day.

2.3 Numbers — and the rules that make floats safe

Prime simulates in IEEE 754 binary32 and binary64. That is safe, and it is safe for a reason worth stating precisely rather than trusting: +, -, *, / and sqrt are exactly specified by IEEE 754 and produce identical results on every conformant platform. Everything else is where determinism dies.

RuleWhy
Only + - * / and sqrt come from the platformExactly specified. Nothing else is.
Every transcendental comes from the console's own librarysin, cos, exp, pow, atan2 are not specified to the last bit and differ between libms and between CPU vendors. Prime ships its own, as normative code, exactly as the small console ships a committed sine table.
Fused multiply-add is MUST NOT unless explicitA compiler contracting a*b+c into an FMA changes the result. It must be an opcode the cart asked for, never an optimisation.
No fast-math, no reassociation, no flush-to-zeroAll three trade exactness for speed, which is the wrong trade in the one place it cannot be made.
NaN payloads are canonicalized at every ABI boundaryThe one place WebAssembly is deliberately non-deterministic.
Iteration order is specified everywhereHash-map order is the classic silent desync. Sort before you iterate, or use an ordered container.
Decision — floats, not fixed point

The small console uses 16.16 fixed point because a script interpreter was a valid runtime path and "mostly deterministic" is worthless when it is. Prime's payload is WebAssembly, whose float semantics are specified, so the fixed-point tax buys nothing and costs a great deal at physics scale. The rules above are what make that safe, and they are enforceable: a conformance runner can diff two implementations over a long replay and find any violation.

2.4 Randomness

One console-owned PRNG, named and specified bit-for-bit: PCG64-DXSM, 128-bit state, seeded by the console, its state living inside the simulation arena and therefore inside every snapshot. There is no other entropy source in the machine.

Why a different generator from the small console

The small console uses xoshiro128** because JavaScript has no 64-bit integers and a 64-bit multiply there is a correctness hazard in the most determinism-critical function in the machine. WebAssembly has i64 natively, so that constraint is gone and the better generator is simply available. The lesson that generalises is the one that produced the change: pick the generator the runtime can express exactly, and write the choice down to the last constant.

2.5 Input

per player, per tick:
  buttons   u16      16 digital, one bit each
  axes      i16 x 4  two sticks, full range, ALREADY QUANTIZED
  triggers  u8  x 2
                     = 12 bytes per player

8 player slots        = 96 bytes per tick
                      = 5.6 KB/s, and a ten-minute replay compresses to a few hundred KB

Analog input MUST be quantized by the runtime before the simulation sees it, at the resolution above. A stick's true position is an analog signal that differs between controllers, driver stacks and polling rates; what reaches the cart is an integer, and integers replay.

Pointer and motion input are extensions (section 4.4), not core, because they cannot be quantized into a fixed-size frame without deciding a resolution, and that decision belongs to a game rather than to the machine.

System buttons — pause, menu, screenshot, record — belong to the console. A cart MUST NOT be able to observe or suppress them.

2.6 Simulation state

The complete state of a running simulation is one contiguous arena, at most 64 MB, plus nothing. No state in host objects, no state in the renderer, no state on the audio thread, no state in a closure that outlives a snapshot.

Decision — mutable state and assets are different memory

This is the change that lets rollback scale. The small console's rule is "the whole machine is one buffer", and at 64 KB that is free. At Prime scale a game may hold gigabytes of textures, meshes and audio, and none of it is mutable: assets are loaded, never written, and identical on every machine running the same cart.

So they are not in the arena. A snapshot copies the arena and nothing else, because immutable data does not need saving — it needs only to be the same, which the cart hash already guarantees. 64 MB at 60 Hz is 3.8 GB/s naive and a small fraction of that with XOR-delta, which is what makes eight frames of rollback affordable on a machine holding twelve gigabytes of assets.

2.7 The simulation runs on one thread

Rendering, audio, asset streaming, decompression and I/O are parallel and non-normative. The simulation is single-threaded, because deterministic multithreading is a research problem wearing an engineering costume, and a specification that pretended otherwise would be a specification with an untestable clause in it.

A deterministic job graph — a fixed schedule the runtime executes identically on every machine — is reserved, defined here and implemented later or never. Reserving it costs a paragraph; adding it after carts exist is a Tier A break.

Tier B

3. The machine

Blast radius: every cart. A change here is a new Prime major, with the old runtime kept alive for the carts that shipped against it.

3.1 The render target

A cart draws in a 1920 x 1080 logical coordinate space using floating-point coordinates. The runtime presents at whatever the display is: 1080p one-to-one, 4K supersampled or integer-scaled, ultrawide with the cart's declared letterbox policy, a handheld at 720p.

Why a logical space rather than a resolution

Pinning a resolution ties every cart to the display generation it shipped in. A logical space means a cart written today renders natively on hardware that does not exist yet, and it makes the resolution question — which is where "modern console" arguments usually start — a property of the runtime rather than of the format.

The number is 1920 x 1080 rather than something smaller because it is the resolution people author art against, and 16:9 because a letterbox is a decision a game should make rather than inherit.

3.2 Colour

8-bit sRGB output, compositing in linear space. HDR10 and Rec. 2020 are declared extensions. There is no palette, no colour count, and no fixed number of anything on screen.

3.4 Audio

Real audio: streamed and static sources, a mixer graph, sample-accurate scheduling, and effects. All of it non-normative, as on the small console and for the same reason — an audio device runs on its own clock, which drifts against the presentation clock and differs per device.

The simulation emits audio commands; the mixer consumes them; nothing flows back. A cart MUST NOT be able to read the audio clock, a playback position, or an analysis of its own output into simulation state.

3.5 Budgets

Gotcha — the tick budget is the one that bites

At 60 Hz a tick has 16.7 ms, and a naive reading says the simulation may use most of it. It may not: presentation, audio and streaming share the frame, and rollback netplay re-simulates up to eight ticks inside one frame. A simulation that uses 8 ms is a game that cannot roll back. Budget accordingly, and measure the re-simulation cost rather than the single-tick cost.

Tier C

4. The ABI

Blast radius: carts using a new call. Additive only within a major.

4.1 A cart submits a scene; it does not touch the GPU

Drawing is a display list built during render and handed to the runtime: transforms, sprites, meshes, text, materials, lights, post-process stages. The runtime decides how to draw it — GPU on a desktop, a software rasterizer on a reference implementation, something else on hardware nobody has built yet.

Decision — this is what makes the sandbox survivable

A cart that could issue raw GPU commands would need a driver, and a driver is an enormous attack surface reachable by a stranger's code. A cart that submits a scene needs nothing but memory. The same choice is what lets a headless conformance runner exist at all: it executes the simulation and simply never rasterizes, which is the only way to verify a million ticks in a CI job.

4.2 Shaders

Carts MAY ship shaders, compiled from a portable IR at stamp time and validated by the runtime. They are presentation only: a shader cannot write to the simulation arena, cannot be read back by the simulation, and a cart MUST NOT be able to branch on whether one compiled.

4.3 The surface

NamespaceWhat it does

Gotcha — asset residency is a determinism trap

Streaming is non-normative, so a cart MUST NOT branch on whether an asset has finished loading. That is exactly the tempting thing to do, and it makes the simulation a function of disk speed. res reports residency to render only; the simulation is told that an asset exists and nothing about where it is.

4.4 Extensions

Pointer, motion, haptics, HDR, ultrawide, more than 8 players. Declared in META as uses (soft, enhance if present) or requires (hard, refuse to run without), the same split glTF uses.

Anything that reaches the simulation — pointer and motion do — MUST define its quantization in the extension, or a replay is not portable between two machines that both claim to support it.

Tier D

5. Carts at scale

Blast radius: tooling and distribution. Forward-compatible by design.

5.1 The container

The same shape as the small console's: magic, version, a chunk directory, typed chunks, uppercase critical and lowercase ancillary so an old runtime refuses exactly the carts it genuinely cannot run and skips the rest. Sizes are 64-bit and the directory is at the head so a chunk can be range-requested without the file.

5.2 Identity is a Merkle root

leaves      1 MiB of canonical cart bytes each
node        BLAKE3 of its children
cart_id     the root, 256 bits, Crockford base32
Decision — BLAKE3 here, and SHA-256 on the small console

The small console specified BLAKE3 and the implementation changed it to SHA-256, because a second hash function is a second thing that can disagree between implementations and a 64 KB cart hashes in about a millisecond either way.

At 16 GB the argument reverses, and not because of speed. A tree hash lets a player verify a one-megabyte leaf the moment it arrives instead of after the whole download, which is what makes streaming a cart safe rather than hopeful: a corrupted or substituted block is caught at the block, by the machine, before anything executes it. BLAKE3 is a tree hash natively. That is a property SHA-256 does not have, and it is the reason — speed is a rounding error next to it.

5.3 Streaming, signing, and what identity covers

A cart MAY be played before it has fully arrived: the runtime verifies each leaf against the Merkle path as it lands. An ed25519 signature over the root establishes the author. The root covers every byte except the signature itself, label art and recipe included — swapping a screenshot on a known cart MUST produce a different id.

5.4 What a cart may not contain

No executable outside the declared WebAssembly. No native code, no dynamic library, no interpreter for a second language shipped as data and fed with cart-authored source. The payload is the payload, and it is the thing that was validated at stamp time.

Tiers E and F

6. Engines and modules

Blast radius: carts that opt in. Free to evolve, because a cart pins a version and the engine is payload inside it.

Inherited unchanged from the small console, because the design never depended on the machine being small. Modules are content-addressed, versioned, and declare what they provide and what they require, so a recipe can be type-checked before anything is built.

One rule earned the hard way and worth carrying across: a manifest is a claim, and the stamper's job is to verify claims against bytes. A pack that declares a capability must be checked for it, not believed.

What Prime adds is scale: modules may carry gigabytes, so a module's identity is a Merkle root and a recipe pins roots rather than paths.

7. The stamper

Unchanged in shape: resolve, merge, bind, validate, compile, pack, prove, seven pure functions, no shared mutable state. The recipe is the source; the cart is a build artifact; the same recipe and the same module roots produce a byte-identical cart on any machine.

7.1 What prove means now

The prover runs the cart headless — simulation only, never rasterizing — for a declared number of ticks against a replay, and records the chain of simulation-state hashes. That chain is the conformance artifact, and it is what a second implementation must reproduce.

Why the prover never draws

It could not usefully: pixels are non-normative, so a rendered frame proves nothing about conformance. It also means proving is cheap — a million ticks in a CI job with no GPU, no display and no driver — which is the difference between a conformance suite that runs on every commit and one that runs when somebody remembers.

8. Security

A first-party console still runs code from a stranger the moment it runs code from a contractor, a middleware vendor, or its own team eighteen months from now. The threat model does not require an open store to be worth having.

Tampered or substituted cartMerkle root is identity, verified per leaf during streaming

Cart code runs in its own process with no filesystem, no network and no device access, communicating with the runtime over a typed channel. The save region is reached through host hooks the cart cannot enumerate.

9. Conformance

An implementation is Prime 1 conformant if and only if:

  1. For every published conformance case, the chain of per-tick simulation-state hashes matches the published chain exactly.
  2. It implements the full Tier C ABI at the minor it declares, and refuses a cart requiring a minor it does not.
  3. It enforces the arithmetic rules of 2.3 — verifiable by running the published float-conformance cart, which exercises every edge the rules exist to protect.
  4. It quantizes input before the simulation sees it, at the resolution in 2.5.
  5. It never exposes wall-clock time, network, host storage or asset residency to the simulation.
  6. It maps the simulation arena read-only during render, or detects a write in development builds.
  7. It refuses carts with unknown critical chunks and accepts carts with unknown ancillary ones.

Nothing about the picture appears in that list, and that is the point.

10. The two consoles

Context rather than requirement: Prime carries no obligation to run a Square One cart, and no cart is required to exist in both places. Porting is a decision made per game, in-house.

What is worth knowing is what the shared architecture gives away for free when a game does exist in both.

A replay crosses the tiers

Because the simulation is the normative boundary on both machines, a cart whose mechanics are the same in both places has the same simulation in both places — and therefore the same replay format, the same scores, the same ghosts and the same netplay. The free console and Prime are then one game at two fidelities rather than two games, and a player's progress and records travel with them.

That is a property of the architecture, not a feature anyone has to build. It only holds where the mechanics genuinely match, which is a design decision per game — but where it holds, it holds completely.

The constraint that decides whether it holds is worth stating once: downlevelling works on assets and never on mechanics. A palette quantizes and a texture downscales; nothing mechanically turns a game built around eight analog axes and two hundred entities into one with eight buttons. A game that is meant to exist on both is designed core-loop-first for the small machine and given fidelity on Prime. A game that is not simply is not.

11. Open questions

  • The normative math library is the critical path. Every transcendental the simulation may use, specified to the bit, with a reference implementation and conformance vectors. Nothing else in Tier A is as much work or as unforgiving, and no cart can ship before it exists.
  • Wasm SIMD. Deterministic in principle; the question is whether every backend agrees today. Until that is measured, SIMD in the simulation is an open risk rather than a decision.
  • The 64 MB arena cap is a guess. It is chosen to keep eight frames of rollback affordable. Whether a real game fits is an empirical question and the answer changes the number.
  • Save migration. A 16 MB save written by version 1 of a cart, read by version 4. The small console never had to answer this; Prime does.
  • Whether the deterministic job graph is ever built, and what it costs to leave the door open.
  • The name. Prime is a working title.

PhaseDeliverableWhy here

Why netplay is last and why it is the acceptance test

Rollback is the most demanding consumer of determinism there is: it re-runs the recent past dozens of times a second and compares against a machine it does not control. A hole that a replay would surface after ten minutes, rollback surfaces in a second, loudly, in front of two players. Building it last and treating it as the measurement — rather than as a feature — is what tells you whether Tier A was actually right.
